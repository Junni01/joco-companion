# The Company — Standalone Strategy Game Concept

**Status:** Exploratory concept, 24 October 2026. Not an approved product specification and not part of the current companion implementation.
**Working title:** The Company (placeholder)
**Relationship to existing project:** A possible future, independent single-player game inspired by historical trading-company governance and the emergent narrative ideas developed for the John Company India Companion. This is not a proposal to change John Company: Second Edition's rules or the current companion's scope.

## 1. Elevator pitch

Run a powerful eighteenth-century chartered trading company whose officials, investors, military commanders and overseas partners are **autonomous people**, not obedient menu options. Set strategy, appoint personnel, issue orders, manage political pressure and decide when to intervene personally. Meanwhile, Indian rulers pursue their own agendas, your employees develop interests of their own, and consequential decisions are remembered across generations.

**Central question:** How much authority are you willing to delegate to effective people when their success may make them difficult to control?

The game's distinguishing feature would be a tight loop between simulation and memory: economic and political outcomes change relationships, and those relationships influence later decisions. The history of each campaign is generated by actual play, then narrated through letters, court reports, memoranda, personal reactions and a dry chronicler.

## 2. Core design pillars

1. **Manage an institution, not every unit.** The player sets priorities and appoints agents; most local decisions occur under delegated authority.
2. **Attention is scarce.** Each turn contains more important matters than the player can personally address. Interventions have an opportunity cost.
3. **People are capable and self-interested.** Employees can be competent, loyal, cautious, corrupt, ruthless, proud or ambitious in combinations that create trade-offs.
4. **India is independently simulated.** Rulers, dynasties and political actors pursue their own objectives, including alliances and conflicts that do not involve the Company.
5. **Information is imperfect but investigable.** Reports may be incomplete, biased, mistaken or deceptive. Players need tools to develop confidence in intelligence.
6. **Commercial expansion is not synonymous with conquest.** Diplomacy, patronage, trade privileges and a smaller sustainable Company can be viable paths.
7. **The narrative is causal and persistent.** A betrayal or favour matters years later because it changes character trust, not because a writer forces the next scripted quest.
8. **History has ethical weight.** Extraction, famine, coercion and war have human consequences, treated seriously rather than as amusing efficiency puzzles.

## 3. Player role and scale

The player controls the Company's central policy-making authority—perhaps a composite Chairman/Board viewpoint rather than one immortal historical character. Exact office framing is open.

### Player controls

- Company priorities: dividends, trade expansion, security, political influence, territorial ambitions, fiscal restraint.
- Senior appointments, promotions, dismissal and succession planning.
- Large expenditures, borrowing, dividends and shareholder policy.
- Diplomatic mandates and major treaty approval.
- Military authorisation and strategic priorities.
- Investigation, discipline, audit and exceptional intervention.
- Relations with Parliament, Crown and investors.

### Delegated activity

- Everyday revenue collection and provincial administration.
- Trade operations, shipping and local contracts.
- Field command and tactical decisions.
- Routine diplomacy, hiring and patronage.
- Intelligence gathering and reporting.

The player can sometimes give more explicit instructions, but direct control consumes scarce attention and may undermine an agent's initiative.

## 4. Proposed annual turn loop

**Time unit is open:** One year is a useful initial proposal; seasons could later support maritime trade, war campaigning and changing markets without turning the game into real-time micromanagement.

1. **Reports arrive:** Financial returns, diplomatic developments, unrest, intelligence, institutional letters, personnel concerns.
2. **Council phase:** Review urgent matters, adjust policy, allocate scarce interventions, budgets and assignments.
3. **Delegation:** Officials interpret mandates through their competence, personality, loyalties and knowledge. Some may exceed authority.
4. **World resolution:** Trade, diplomacy, warfare, politics and independent Indian-state decisions advance; uncertainty and delays apply.
5. **Consequences:** Revenue, reputation, territorial influence, relationships and institutional control change.
6. **Narrative and memory:** The game records verified outcomes, private interpretations, promises, grudges and longer arcs, creating a chapter for the year.

### Attention system

A small pool of **major interventions** per turn is an initial design candidate, not settled balance (for example, five interventions amid ten pressing matters). Potential uses: investigate a Governor, negotiate personally, dictate a military objective, veto a local arrangement, dismiss an official or negotiate Parliamentary support.

Unattended matters are not automatically failures. Agents act according to policy, competence, initiative and their own agenda. This makes delegation genuinely useful and risky.

Open balancing questions: Is attention a hard action-point limit, a fatigue/political-capital cost, or a system of competing time commitments? Can high-quality institutional processes reduce routine attention demand?

## 5. Characters and autonomy

### Important actor types

| Actor | Agency and pressures |
| --- | --- |
| Governors and regional administrators | Revenue, patronage, order, private wealth, local legitimacy |
| Army commanders | Military reputation, officer networks, security, autonomy |
| Merchants and shipping officials | Profit, risk tolerance, trade access, private dealing |
| Accountants and secretaries | Institutional memory, audits, information and career |
| Directors and shareholders | Dividends, appointments, financial survival, factional interests |
| Parliament and Crown representatives | Legitimacy, national interests, political control |
| Indian rulers and claimants | Security, succession, legitimacy, trade and sovereignty |
| Foreign rivals and clients | Commercial privileges, strategic competition, political patronage |

Characters have competence (domain skills), enduring traits (ambition, pride, caution, ruthlessness, loyalty tendency, vindictiveness), short-term moods, personal connections, interests, obligations and historical memory. The numbers beneath these may be hidden, while the interface communicates plausible tendencies.

**An employee is neither always obedient nor randomly disloyal.** Their action policy should weigh:
- The player's stated mandate and available resources.
- Competence, information and practical constraints.
- Personal ambition, reputation, risk tolerance and loyalties.
- Incentives and supervision.
- Relationships with local political actors and other Company staff.

A brilliant Governor may become powerful and independent precisely because the Company depends on their effectiveness. An honest but ineffective Governor may also pose a serious problem.

### Example: The indispensable Governor

A capable Governor increases Bengal revenue and suppresses immediate threats. Over time he forms personal networks, cultivates local clients, becomes wealthy and increasingly ignores London. Removing him risks destabilising the region; retaining him risks ceding more authority. Promoting or transferring him may solve one problem and create another. Neither betrayal nor downfall is guaranteed.

## 6. Institutional versus personal memory

Offices retain formal treaties, precedents, accounts, reports and chains of authority. People retain friendships, private deals, resentments and knowledge not always written down.

When a respected Governor dies, a successor receives official records but not the trust that made a treaty workable. This creates succession crises without requiring a random rebellion card. Succession planning, record keeping and knowledge transfer become substantive strategies.

Data concepts:
- **Factual world event:** What objectively happened in the simulation.
- **Institutional record:** What the Company has recorded or believes happened, including omissions and reliability.
- **Character interpretation:** A personal view or accusation, sometimes disputed.
- **Relationship consequence:** How an actor's trust, loyalty, fear or hostility changes.
- **Narrative arc:** A persistent, unresolved thread and its possible future reactions.

## 7. Imperfect information and reporting

The player sees the world partly through dispatches, financial reports, rumours, petitions, spies and advisers. Reports differ because of delay, error, incentive, political framing or deliberate deception.

Example: A Governor declares Bengal peaceful and revenue healthy. An accountant confirms increased revenue but reports that tax collection now requires more soldiers. A regional ruler warns of resentment. The player can trust a source, compare evidence, commission an inquiry or intervene.

Design safeguards:
- Reports carry source, age, confidence and potential conflict of interest.
- Investigation can improve knowledge; uncertainty is not arbitrary fog.
- Players can learn which officials are reliable over time.
- The game should distinguish false beliefs from intentional lies.
- Some information becomes verifiable after events, allowing accountability and meaningful callbacks.

## 8. An independent Indian political simulation

Indian states should not exist merely as Company targets. Their rulers have distinct objectives, domestic constituencies, military and economic capacities, dynastic claims and independent diplomacy. They may trade, form coalitions, challenge succession, negotiate protection, fight neighbours, invite foreign assistance or reject intervention even while the Company is passive.

Political relationships and motives matter mechanically. A ruler betrayed during succession may distrust the Company in future talks; a ruler rescued from exile may be grateful but later resent dependence. Successors can inherit the knowledge of family grievances without automatically inheriting identical personalities.

**Choice illustration:** A ruler's brother claims Hyderabad's throne. Supporting the incumbent may protect an honoured agreement; backing the ambitious rival may improve immediate trade privileges; neutrality may preserve legitimacy but sacrifice leverage. Subsequent outcomes are driven by the simulation and actors, not a prewritten branch.

## 9. Economy and trade

The economy should make the Company feel like a commercial institution first.

Candidate systems:
- Trade routes, ports, warehouses, charter privileges and regional commodity access.
- Commercial ventures, convoy risk, shipping capacity and seasonal disruptions.
- Working capital, loans, debt service, dividends, share confidence and credit.
- Administrative expense, garrisons and the often high cost of territorial rule.
- Tax revenue versus social legitimacy and risk of revolt.
- Corruption, private trade and loss of revenue to local officials.
- Home-government policy, regulation, war and monopoly rights.

Avoid a sprawling production-chain puzzle initially. Model a few important commodities and financial constraints well enough that trade partnerships, military conquest and fiscal restraint offer genuinely different strategies.

Open questions: Which commodities and markets? Is pricing simulated locally, globally or abstractly? How do Indian producers and communities respond? How much operational detail is fun versus busywork?

## 10. War, diplomacy and expansion

The player sets objectives, recruits or funds forces, chooses commanders and negotiates alliances. Commanders execute plans with competence, initiative and agendas. Tactical combat might be abstracted to preserve focus on political administration rather than unit manoeuvres.

Conquest yields strategic advantages but introduces governance costs, legitimacy problems, rival claimants and administrative obligations. Foreign commercial/military powers compete through support for local clients as well as direct conflict.

Possible strategic paths:
- A profitable trading company with limited territorial control.
- An alliance-based web of privileges and protected partners.
- A powerful but expensive territorial empire.
- A highly regulated, government-backed institution.
- A diminished but surviving commercial enterprise.

None should be automatically the sole optimal way to win.

## 11. London politics, incentives and loss of autonomy

Different institutions pursue conflicting goals:

| Actor | Typical demands |
| --- | --- |
| Shareholders | Dividends, stable profits, share value |
| Company directors | Independence, patronage, long-term corporate survival |
| Parliament | Accountability, national strategy, political leverage |
| Crown | Prestige, security and stability |
| Employees | Careers, salary, influence, wealth and protection |
| Indian rulers and communities | Sovereignty, security, legitimate rule, economic opportunity |

A profitable but scandal-ridden Company may face intervention. A Company rescued from bankruptcy may have to surrender its charter privileges. A military success may worsen fiscal stability. Company legitimacy and institutional autonomy become resources distinct from profit or territory.

## 12. Emergent narrative presentation

The dry, adaptive chronicler provides accurate historical framing. Letters and conversations carry character-specific interests and perceptions. A yearly chapter connects events into larger threads without inventing a cause.

Potential story arcs:
- The indispensable but disobedient Governor.
- An exiled dynasty returning through a local rebellion.
- A trusted secretary exposing corruption.
- A military commander forming private loyalties.
- A Company-backed ruler gradually rejecting foreign dependence.
- A diplomatic friendship collapsing after personnel succession.
- A commercial triumph producing a political catastrophe.

Dialogue is initially best generated from structured, authored templates with factual predicates and durable callbacks. Live generative AI is optional research for the distant future, not a core requirement. Character memories should influence simulation decisions in the standalone game—**unlike the cosmetic-only companion**.

## 13. Outcomes and victory

Consider a campaign spanning decades, with multiple possible outcomes rather than a simple map-painting victory:
- Sustainable, commercially prosperous Company.
- Expansive territorial Company with high financial/political burden.
- Company subordinated to Parliament or effectively nationalised.
- Bankruptcy or charter loss.
- Strategic retreat from India and survival in trade.
- Political or military expulsion from important markets.

Possible score dimensions: financial success, institutional independence, stability, political influence, durable relationships and human consequences. Exact win conditions are unresolved. A morally complicated historical simulation should not present exploitation as a consequence-free reward.

## 14. Risks and design safeguards

1. **Too much automation:** Ensure the player has meaningful policy, staffing and crisis choices and can understand why agents acted.
2. **Too many characters:** Focus on a manageable prominent cast, with deeper simulated background and filters.
3. **Opaque randomness:** Give causes, warning signals and investigative choices; failures should be explainable retrospectively.
4. **Runaway instability:** Prevent feedback loops that leave the player helpless; allow repair, delegation and strategic retreat.
5. **Narrative/mechanical contradiction:** Generate narration from committed facts; mark opinions and misinformation as such.
6. **Feature explosion:** Prove attention, delegation and character incentives before adding hundreds of commodities, states or portraits.
7. **Historical sensitivity:** Research local institutions and economic conditions; avoid ethnic stereotypes, deterministic cultural traits and treating civilian harm as comedic spectacle.
8. **Intellectual-property independence:** Build an original game identity, rules and presentation. Treat the existing board-game companion as a technical and learning prototype, not a digital recreation of proprietary content.

## 15. Relationship to the current John Company companion

The companion remains a faithful board-game assistant with narrative-only consequences. Any standalone game should live in a **separate project/repository**, with its own systems, terminology, assets and design authority.

Potentially reusable ideas/code, subject to refactoring and rights review:
- Static web architecture and map rendering.
- Character identities, traits, claims, relationships and story arcs.
- Incident log and factual narrative safeguards.
- Authored dialogue selection and chronicler.
- Save/Undo architecture and printable campaign history.
- Some general UI patterns for dossiers, event scenes and history.

Not directly reusable as authoritative rules:
- John Company event tiles, legal target rules, dice formulas and loot mechanics.
- Physical player-family and office rules.
- The companion's specific scenario setups and rule-driven chronology.

The standalone game's primary missing systems would be economy, autonomous agent policy, diplomacy, war, imperfect information and the player's governance/attention system.

## 16. Early prototypes worth testing

**Prototype A — The Council:** No map or battles yet. A small roster of autonomous Governors, five interventions per year, imperfect reports, policy mandates and personnel changes. Can this alone produce interesting choices?

**Prototype B — The Political Triangle:** Three Indian states, one Company Presidency, two external patrons. Model claims, relationships, promises, succession and diplomatic choices. Test whether historical memory generates credible conflict and reconciliation.

**Prototype C — Profit versus legitimacy:** One province, simple trade and taxes, administrator autonomy, unrest and fiscal pressure. Find whether the player faces genuine trade-offs rather than a dominant strategy.

**Prototype D — Chronicle playback:** Simulate ten to twenty years using authored templates and inspect whether the resulting biography and political history are coherent, varied and grounded.

Prototype order is open; A is the most direct test of the game's distinctive premise.

## 17. Questions for future design sessions

1. Is the player an individual Chairman, the Board, or a continuing institutional perspective that outlives officeholders?
2. How much authority is delegated by default and what can an intervention override?
3. Should turns be yearly, seasonal, or a hybrid?
4. What information must remain uncertain, and what investigative tools are available?
5. How many characters, Indian polities and foreign factions can the player meaningfully follow?
6. How independent are employee decisions: strict policy rules, weighted utility, or a hybrid?
7. How do appointments and accountability work when talented people become politically entrenched?
8. What level of commercial detail creates real dilemmas without an operations spreadsheet?
9. Should war be resolved abstractly, through strategic orders, or with tactical interaction?
10. What is the geographical and chronological scope beyond the existing eight regions?
11. Can the Company succeed without territorial conquest, and what constitutes victory?
12. How are historical oppression, famine, coerced labour and regional autonomy represented responsibly?
13. What is the minimum visually compelling interface before portraits and art?
14. How much of the procedural narrative needs handcrafted content versus flexible templates?
15. Which of the proposed prototypes should be built first?

## 18. Technical direction and architecture — agreed starting approach

**Decision:** Build the initial standalone game using web technologies, but **not** as an unstructured collection of plain HTML/JavaScript files. Use a separate repository and a modular application architecture. The game remains a future project; this is a technology direction, not an implementation start.

### Proposed stack

| Layer | Initial approach | Possible later extension |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Simulation and game rules | TypeScript library independent of rendering | Web Workers for expensive simulation, profiling-driven optimisation |
| User interface | React + TypeScript for council, dispatches, dossiers, reports and event scenes | Refined strategy-game UI and accessibility |
| Political map | SVG with interactive region geometry | PixiJS/WebGL if animated map effects or large-scale rendering warrant it |
| Narrative engine | Separate TypeScript module consuming committed game incidents and feeding character state back into simulation | Larger authored catalogue and more sophisticated procedural selection |
| Persistence | Local campaign saves, versioned schema, JSON import/export | IndexedDB or other local database if save complexity demands it |
| Distribution | Browser first | Desktop packaging later without a simulation rewrite |
| Tests | Deterministic unit/integration tests and simulation batch tests | Balance simulations, performance and long-campaign regression |

No live service or online AI is required for the initial game. Avoid a backend until a concrete need—such as multiplayer, cloud sync or server-run campaigns—justifies it. Desktop distribution can be assessed independently of the rendering stack.

### Separation of responsibilities

The **headless simulation** owns economy, diplomatic state, character decision policies, governance, military outcomes, time and random seeds. It exposes commands and serialisable results, never imports UI components or browser document APIs.

The **narrative engine** consumes committed factual incidents, maintains personal/institutional memory, chooses contextually eligible dialogue and produces chronicle material. Unlike the current board-game companion, character memories and relationships can legitimately influence *future autonomous simulation decisions*; narration still cannot retroactively rewrite committed facts.

The **React presentation layer** displays reports, political information, maps and decisions, and dispatches player commands. It does not implement independent versions of economy or diplomacy rules. SVG is sufficient at first; animations and GPU effects are optional.

The **persistence layer** versions saves, restores complete simulation/narrative state and deterministic random outcomes, and supports debugging or replay. Prevent changing history merely by reopening a scene or report.

### Performance and scalability

The initial scope is dozens or hundreds of meaningful characters and a moderate number of political/economic entities. This is a design target, **not** a measured performance claim. Profile before adopting a game engine or introducing worker threads. Avoid per-frame simulation; the world can advance in discrete turns or stages.

Crucially, support a command-line or automated test harness that runs the simulation **without the UI**. One desired capability is to simulate 100 fifty-year campaigns with autonomous Governors and independent Indian rulers, then report bankruptcy, trade profitability, state stability and territorial expansion. Such batch tests will expose runaway feedback loops and dominant strategies well before visual polish.

### When to reconsider technology

React/SVG is the preferred baseline for a primarily map-and-interface-driven political game. Reconsider a specialist rendering layer (PixiJS or Phaser) if animated armies, ships, visual effects and interactive 2D scenes become central. Reconsider a full engine such as Godot if the project evolves toward continuous animation, detailed tactical play or a heavily scene-driven presentation. A technology switch should follow a tested feature need, not an assumption that HTML cannot support a strategy game.

### Architectural acceptance criteria

1. A deterministic, seeded simulation run completes without a browser or graphical interface.
2. UI controls issue commands to shared simulation rules; they do not duplicate or bypass those rules.
3. Character decisions can be inspected with explanations of mandates, incentives, knowledge and relationship factors.
4. Narrative output is grounded in committed events, while memories affect only later decisions.
5. Saving and reloading reproduces identical world, character and narrative state.
6. SVG map rendering and React dossiers remain replaceable without rewriting the simulation.
7. Run long-campaign batch tests to measure balance and performance before investing heavily in graphics.

## 19. Status and next step

**This is an idea incubator, not a scheduled development project.** The web/TypeScript/React/SVG starting architecture has been agreed as a direction; no standalone mechanics, final setting scope, name, complete UI or implementation have been approved. The current John Company companion's narrative implementation remains a separate priority.

When returning to this concept, first choose the player role and test **attention + delegation + imperfect reports** with a deliberately small cast. Preserve alternatives, playtest the core tension, and expand only if the basic experience is engaging.
